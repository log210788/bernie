Protocol 1 • Perimeter Safety

The Urgent Imperative: Why Courtesy Can Be Dangerous

In regular English conversation, polite softening ("Would you mind stepping back please?") is prized. But on a disaster perimeter with cracked masonry, politeness registers as low urgency. Bystanders assume there is time to argue.

International teams use the loud imperative: verb first, no padding: "Step back! Keep clear of the building!". It snaps survivors out of shock and triggers immediate compliance.

Protocol 2 • Acoustic Search

The Silence Discipline: Detecting Tapping in the Rubble

Trapped survivors frequently have dry throats from dust or broken ribs that prevent loud screaming. Their only way to communicate is tapping with a stone or pipe, or blowing a whistle from an emergency kit.

Before calling out, order total perimeter silence: "Everyone quiet! We need to listen!". Then shout: "Can anyone hear me? Make noise if you can!", followed by 30 seconds of absolute stillness.

Protocol 3 • Logistics Clarity

Safe Zone vs. Shelter: Clearing Up the Field Distinction

As Louie and Bernie discussed today, confusing these two terms causes chaos for arriving convoys.

A Safe Zone (*zona segura*) is an immediate, open outdoor gathering point (a stadium pitch or public park) free of secondary collapse hazards. A Shelter (*albergue / refugio*) is a long-term covered facility equipped with cots, latrines, clean water, and food rations where displaced families will sleep.

Protocol 4 • Psychological First Aid

Verbal Grounding: Preventing Panic in Pinned Victims

Bernie made an exceptional point in today's class: talking continuously to trapped victims prevents nervous breakdowns. When pinned in total darkness, silence feels like abandonment.

Use the reassuring verbal triad: "Help is on the way. Stay calm, do not move, and breathe." Ask them what they see (light, pipes, room color) to give search dogs and fiber-optic cameras a target vector while maintaining emotional contact.
